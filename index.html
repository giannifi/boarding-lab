    How the model works

    Agents in a lane. Every aisle is a line of cells, one per row, and only one person fits in a cell. A passenger stowing a bag blocks everyone behind them. These aisle interferences are the main cause of slow boarding.
    Bags and bins. Each row has limited bin space. Stowing gets slower as bins fill. When the bin at your row is full, you use one up to 3 rows away. When nothing is free nearby, the bag is gate-checked.
    Seat shuffles. When someone already seated sits between you and your seat, they have to stand up and step out. The cost is a fixed part plus a part for each neighbour who gets up.
    Real-world effects. Pre-boarding, travel parties who walk on together, passengers who ignore their group call, slow walkers, fare and status tiers, and load factors below 100%.
    Disruptions. Optional irregular events: someone in the wrong seat, a bag sent to the hold, a seat-swap negotiation, crew assistance, a medical incident, a boarding pause, late passengers, and a no-show whose bag must be offloaded. They are drawn per seed, so every method faces the same events. The Risk tab compares clean and disrupted boarding.
    Calibration. With default parameters, the 12-row research cabin reproduces the measured ranking of the Steffen & Hotchkiss (2011) experiment, and each method's mean is within about 13% of its measured time. The experiment's own single-run uncertainty is about 10%.
    Limits. This is a planning model, not a digital twin. It leaves out jet-bridge queues, gate scanning and people passing each other in the aisle. Use it to compare options and understand sensitivities, and validate against your own boarding timestamps.
